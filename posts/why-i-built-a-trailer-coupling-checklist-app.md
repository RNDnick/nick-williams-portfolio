I'd heard about dropped trailers before I ever drove one myself — the kind of story that gets told in yards and canteens as a cautionary tale about someone else's bad day. Then it happened twice in the same month at a yard I was working out of, to two different drivers, for two different reasons. That was enough.

## The BLACK method isn't the problem

Every driver knows BLACK — brakes, legs, air lines, clip, kingpin. It's a good method, drilled in from day one. The failures I saw weren't failures to know the steps. They were failures under the ordinary pressure of a shift: rushing because you're already running late, getting interrupted halfway through a check, or genuinely misremembering whether you did step three before you moved on to step four.

A checklist you have to actively fill in fixes some of that. But a paper checklist, or a mental one, still relies on you being honest with yourself in the moment about whether you actually did the step, rather than just believing you did.

## What a photo changes

The idea behind the app is simple: each BLACK step needs a timestamped photo before you can move to the next one. That's it. No scoring, no gamification, nothing clever. Just a forcing function that makes "I did check the airlines" into something you can prove rather than something you remember.

It also turns out to be useful after the fact, in a way I hadn't fully anticipated before building it. If something does go wrong, there's a record — not to catch anyone out, but so a conversation about what happened doesn't have to start from two people's competing memories of a rushed five minutes in a dark yard.

## Building it the same way as the others

Same approach as the rest of the site: one evening, working with Claude Code, focused on the one job the tool needs to do well. No login walls, no upsell, nothing to configure before you can use it on shift. If it saves one dropped trailer, it's paid for the evening it took to build.
