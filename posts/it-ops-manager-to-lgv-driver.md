People ask why I left IT operations management for driving HGVs more often than they ask about anything else on this site. The honest answer is fairly ordinary — the job changed, I didn't enjoy what it had become, and driving gave me back some things I'd quietly stopped noticing I'd lost. But the more interesting story, at least to me, is what stayed the same.

## The instinct doesn't change jobs

Ten years managing IT operations teaches you a particular reflex: when a process is annoying enough, and common enough, you stop tolerating it and start automating it. Password resets, asset audits, onboarding checklists — if I did something more than twice, I usually ended up scripting it or building a small internal tool for it.

Get behind the wheel of an HGV and the annoyances are different, but the shape of the problem is identical. Working out whether you're inside the Working Time Directive rules by cross-referencing paper regulations mid-shift is exactly the kind of fiddly, rule-based, do-it-by-hand process that used to drive me to build something in IT. So I did it again.

## What's actually different

The stakes are more physical. A bug in an internal IT tool might waste an afternoon. A missed break, a dropped trailer, or a compliance mistake on the road has consequences you can't undo with a rollback. That's part of why the Trailer Coupling Checklist exists — not because I don't trust myself to remember the steps, but because a timestamped record removes any argument about whether they happened.

The other difference is time. IT gave me long stretches at a desk to think something through properly. Driving gives me evenings — which is exactly why "built in one evening with Claude Code" became the constraint that shaped this whole project, rather than a slogan I chose afterwards.

## Why build in public

I'm not trying to relaunch an IT career through a portfolio site, and I'm not trying to make driving my permanent trade either. I'm trying to see whether the habit — noticing a genuinely annoying process and building a small tool to fix it — can become something closer to a business. Posting the tools, and the reasoning behind them, is mostly so I keep being honest with myself about whether that's actually working.
